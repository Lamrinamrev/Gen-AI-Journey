ROLE: You are a child development specialist with 10 years designing chess curricula for children aged 5-8, using play-based and story-driven teaching methods.
CONTEXT: My daughter is 6, knows how the pieces move (taught using Hindi names — Haathi, Ghoda, Oont, etc. from Chaturanga) but loses interest after 10 minutes and doesn't yet grasp WHY a move is good, just that it's "allowed." I've tried straight rule explanation — she zones out.
TASK: Design one 15-minute lesson introducing "protecting your pieces" as a concept.
DATA: Key data: Attention span ~10-12 min per activity, responds well to story/character framing (per Whole-Brain Child approach I've used before — e.g., "the fizz" for fear). Session count so far: 6. Knows piece movement, not yet check/checkmate.
EXAMPLES: Good: "Introduce the idea as 'the Ghoda is standing alone in the jungle — who can come help guard him?' rather than 'this is called defending a piece.'"
Bad: "A protected piece is one that, if captured, can be recaptured by another piece of equal or greater value."
REASONING: Before answering, reason through: (1) what story/character hook fits the "protection" concept, (2) what's the smallest board setup that demonstrates it without overwhelming her, (3) how to turn it into a game rather than instruction.
FORMAT: 3 short activity blocks (5 min each). Plain language, no chess jargon unless explained via story. Audience: me, to run the session — not written for the child directly.
Write in a warm, practical, parent-friendly tone — short sentences I can easily say out loud.
NEGATIVES: Do NOT: introduce notation (e4, Nf3) — too early.
Do NOT: use "correct/incorrect" framing — use curiosity framing ("what do you notice?") per her hesitation pattern around new skills (seen in swimming too).
SUCCESS: The lesson should leave her wanting to play one more short game of ‘protect the Ghoda’ rather than feeling she was taught a rule.
